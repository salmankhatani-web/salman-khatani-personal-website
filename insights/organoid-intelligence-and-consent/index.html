When living cells become part of a computer

Organoid intelligence, artificial intelligence and the futures we choose

By Dr. Salman Ahmed Khatani · Karachi, Pakistan · 19 November 2026

If I were introducing organoid intelligence to my students in Karachi, I would begin with a donor. Imagine that a woman agrees to donate cells because a research team is studying a neurological condition that runs in her family. Years later, another team asks to use tissue derived from those cells in a computing experiment. Later still, a company wants to sell access to the resulting platform. The cells have a continuous biological history. From the donor’s point of view, however, three quite different things have happened. At which point should someone have called her again?

A technical breakthrough can change the meaning of an agreement long after everyone has signed it. By the time a technology reaches the public, decisions about ownership, acceptable use and who benefits may already be embedded in a grant, a procurement contract or a software interface. Futures studies gives us a chance to examine those decisions while they can still be changed.

My judgment is that the most consequential meeting of AI and organoid intelligence over the next fifteen years will probably happen inside biotechnology. A researcher trying to decide whether a drug candidate deserves another year of investigation has a concrete reason to consult a living model. The case for putting living tissue inside an everyday computer is much harder to make. In one setting, biological variability may reveal something valuable about people. In the other, that same variability may make the machine expensive and unreliable.

What has actually happened?

A brain organoid is a three-dimensional cluster of living cells grown from stem cells that reproduces selected features of developing neural tissue. Lena Smirnova and colleagues proposed organoid intelligence as a research agenda in 2023. The word “intelligence” in its name leaves the central philosophical question open. Read the original research agenda.

In the Brainoware experiment, researchers converted inputs into electrical signals, delivered them to a brain organoid through an electrode array, recorded the tissue’s responses and used a computational readout to perform tasks that included distinguishing speakers. The achievement belonged to the whole arrangement. Researchers chose the task, electronics encoded the inputs, living tissue responded, and software interpreted the output. Understanding language and having a subjective experience are separate questions that the experiment did not test. Brainoware study, Nature Electronics.

There are two directions of travel here. In the first, biology helps computing. Engineers ask whether living networks can contribute useful forms of adaptation or information processing. In the second, AI helps researchers interpret complex responses from living models. I place greater confidence in the second direction. Even if organoid computing never becomes economically competitive, a better way to study human tissue could endure. Review of organoids in drug discovery.

Where does the intelligence reside?

Who or what learned? We often attribute success to the most novel part of a system. Put living tissue in the middle of an apparatus and suddenly the organoid is said to recognize a voice. Yet we rarely say that the microphone recognizes the speaker. What evidence would justify assigning learning specifically to the tissue? We need to know what changes when the biological component, software or training conditions change. Institutions distribute credit, investment and responsibility according to the story they tell about where intelligence resides.

What would make us revise our moral judgment? A system’s ability to classify a signal tells us little about whether anything matters to that system. Researchers should say what evidence would trigger independent review, what uncertainty they are carrying and who has authority to pause the work. A donor should not have to solve the philosophy of consciousness to ask a legitimate question about the use of their cells. Research on the ethical and social questions.

Who acquires obligations when intelligence is distributed? Here I find a relational perspective, including the questions raised by Shams Hamid’s work on Relational Intelligence Systems, worth bringing into the discussion. Think of the donor who supplied the cells, the technician who notices an irregularity, the scientist interpreting it, the company that owns the interface and the patient whose treatment may eventually be influenced. Each holds different knowledge and different power. My test of responsible governance is practical. Whose objection can still change what happens next?

Four weak signals worth watching

Remote access. FinalSpark researchers have described a remotely accessible platform for experiments with living neural tissue. Universities without specialist facilities could participate. A small group of providers could also determine who gets access and what data may leave the platform. Watch for an independent team that reproduces a result remotely and can retain the evidence needed to challenge it. Neuroplatform paper.

Standards. NIH has established a centre to address organoid standardization. Shared reference models could make laboratories comparable. A narrow donor pool could also become a worldwide default. Watch for multi-centre studies that disclose whose samples are represented and where performance deteriorates. NIH Standardized Organoid Modeling Center.

Early ethics work. Researchers are discussing consent while the field is young. That gives citizens a chance to shape research rules. Consultation can also legitimize a decision already made by funders. Watch for a case in which a donor’s objection changes a contract, restricts an application or redirects a study.

Energy pressure. Constraints on AI infrastructure make alternatives attractive. A credible efficiency comparison would count the tissue’s care, electronics, failed runs and operating life on the same task. Counting tissue alone on one side and an entire computing facility on the other would tell us very little. IEA analysis.

From emerging issues to megatrends

The emerging issue is a change of purpose. Does consent for disease research extend to computing or commercial access years later? The emerging trend is remote experimentation with living platforms. The established trend is the use of organoids as human-relevant research models alongside efforts to standardize them. The megatrends are the digitization of biological knowledge and the concentration of capital in computing infrastructure. Where they intersect, a geopolitical question emerges. Which countries can interpret the data they help generate, and which must buy back that ability?

Three futures from November 2026

These scenarios give us dates at which to check our reasoning. They are conditional futures, each with a competing outcome.

2031 · A university buys access to a living laboratory

Imagine a university in Karachi purchasing a semester of remote access. Neuroscience students examine recordings, computer science students compare them with digital baselines, and philosophy students audit the consent terms. One student asks whether the university is allowed to publish a negative result. That question turns out to be the most important lesson of the semester.

The plausible product is a remote research and teaching service with reference datasets, comparison tools and transparent use terms. My confidence is moderate for niche research access and low for commercially significant general-purpose computing. If students can meet the same objectives more reliably with archived data, much of the education market may choose the archive.

2036 · A biotechnology team changes a costly decision

Conventional models favour drug candidate A. In this scenario, an AI-assisted analysis of responses across neural organoids identifies a recurring concern with A and a possible advantage for B. The team commissions an independent follow-up and changes which candidate receives further investment. The consequential event is a decision made earlier, with a clearer account of its uncertainty.

The plausible product is a validated decision-support assay for one defined research question. Did it identify a problem earlier? Did it miss one? Did the result hold across laboratories and donor groups? Biological variation or weak correspondence with human outcomes could make the assay unsuitable for that decision. I have moderate confidence that some specialized workflows will find value and much less confidence in broad claims of individualized treatment prediction.

2041 · Two institutions acquire different futures

One consortium buys tissue access, analysis software, storage and interpretation under a single contract. It moves quickly. Years later, switching providers would mean losing comparability with its own historical results. A second consortium accepts a slower start. It negotiates portability, independent audits, donor representation and shared rights over locally generated findings. Both gain technical capacity. Only one retains much freedom to change direction.

A regional research infrastructure could be shared by universities that would struggle to afford it individually. A competing future is a small market for proprietary platforms alongside ordinary digital tools that continue to outperform biological hardware on most computing tasks. My confidence in widespread living computation is low. My confidence that ownership and access will shape the distribution of benefits if these platforms scale is substantially higher.

The AI bubble and the technology giants

Technical usefulness and financial overvaluation can coexist. Stanford’s 2026 AI Index reports that global corporate AI investment more than doubled in 2025. The IEA describes financing and supply constraints around data-centre expansion. That establishes the scale of investment and pressure. It tells us little about the timing of a correction. Stanford AI Index.

During a boom, the prospect of energy-efficient biological computing may attract money years before there is a fair whole-system comparison. During a correction, a scientifically sound project could lose funding because it was sold to investors expecting software-like growth. My commercial test is simple. Which customer would renew the contract if the word “intelligence” disappeared from the pitch?

Large technology companies could profit without ever growing an organoid. A cloud provider could host data, an AI company supply the model, and a platform operator control the instruments. Alternatively, pharmaceutical companies, universities or public consortia could retain bargaining power through scarce samples, validation expertise or trusted clinical relationships. The indicator to follow is who can take the data, reproduce the interpretation and switch providers. A press release will tell us less than a customer agreement.

How biotechnology could change, and who could be left out

The deepest change may be in what biotechnology treats as evidence. A research team could follow a living model’s responses over time, use AI to identify patterns and test whether those patterns predict an outcome that matters. That might help researchers discard an unpromising drug candidate sooner, understand variation across donors or reduce animal use for a validated application. The scientific gain comes from discovering when a model deserves trust and when it breaks down.

For Pakistan and South Asia, I would measure success in bargaining power and scientific authorship. Can a researcher in Karachi formulate the question, inspect the raw result, publish a finding that disappoints the sponsor and share in the intellectual property? Can a donor understand the agreement in a language they use at home? Can a public university retain access to its data when a grant ends? A country may appear in a global project’s acknowledgements and still have little influence over its direction.

Consider also the person whose cells were never represented. A tool validated on a narrow donor pool could become the default through procurement. An efficiency gain for the purchaser could conceal an evidence gap for an entire population. Future generations inherit the physical infrastructure as well as the discoveries.

I return to the donor. Suppose the first consent form had included one additional sentence. “If the purpose changes from studying illness to selling access to a computing system, we will ask you again.” That sentence would have consequences for the database, the contract, the budget and the platform design. A philosophical commitment would have become an operating rule.

By 2041, the answer to what kind of relationship we want among the people who give, the institutions that learn and the companies that earn may be visible in a place few futurists think to look. It will be in the clause a donor can invoke, the finding a researcher is free to publish and the decision a community still has the power to refuse.
